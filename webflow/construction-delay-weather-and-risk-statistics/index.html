Do weather days match the rain gauges?


Texas DOT records each working day on a road contract as charged or credited, with a reason for every credit. Mastt matched 2,170 weekdays credited for rain, wet ground or other precipitation against county rain gauge records for the same dates.


On 47% of those days, gauges showed under a tenth of an inch of rain on the day or the day before. On 32% they showed none at all. Over the same period, 85% of ordinary charged weekdays had under a tenth of an inch, so excused days were clearly wetter than normal days. A credited weekday was far more likely to follow rain than a charged one, with an odds ratio of 6.4.

Step 5 · TextText block 3: paste into the post body (Rich Text)276 words
This is a signal, not proof of misuse. County gauges can miss a patchy storm, and Mastt used the median of the gauges in each county, which can read zero on days when only part of a county gets rain. Site conditions can also lag rainfall. A paper on adverse-weather analysis for time extensions by Nguyen and colleagues (Journal of Construction Engineering and Management, 2010) lists lingering days and the definition of normal weather among seven factors that cause discrepancies. In Mastt's data, 26% of credited days had a tenth of an inch of rain two days earlier, against 9% of charged days.


Contract wording shapes how many weather days exist. The TxDOT contract administration handbook says working day charges can be suspended only when work is suspended, and gives hazardous weather at the engineer's direction as one example. It also lists unsuitable weather or soil conditions as a reason work may be suspended while time charges may still apply. Weather does not stop the clock on its own. A decision to suspend work or credit a day has to be recorded.


Other standard forms ask for other evidence. The AIA A201 General Conditions list adverse weather as a cause for extending the contract time only when it is documented with data showing conditions were abnormal for the period, under sections 8.3.1 and 15.1.6.2, as reproduced by Berger Singerman. Holding Redlich notes that AS 4000 and AS 4902 give the contractor no weather relief in their standard form. Projects often amend them to allow an extension of time but not costs, and often require the weather event to be published by the Bureau of Meteorology.

Do wetter seasons mean more late penalties?


If weather days work as intended, a wet season should stretch a job without costing the contractor. Mastt tested this on 7,178 Texas DOT working-day contracts awarded from 2014 to 2024. Each job was ranked against others awarded in the same year by how much wetter or drier its work window was than normal for the same dates in the same county, then split into fifths. The wettest fifth had about 33% more rain days than normal. The driest had about 32% fewer.


The result is flat at the extremes. 12% of jobs in the driest fifth were charged late penalties, and 12% in the wettest fifth. Average penalty days fell from 3.8 in the driest fifth to 3.0 in the wettest. The three middle fifths ran higher, at 16% to 18%, and Mastt could not explain that pattern from these records. Rain did not push penalties up in a straight line.

Step 9 · TextText block 5: paste into the post body (Rich Text)118 words
Calendar-day contracts, which are almost all maintenance, behave differently because every calendar day counts. Between 1.4% and 2.1% of those jobs were charged penalties in each fifth, with no pattern by wetness. In a regression with controls for contract size, award year, district and work type, a wetter work window was linked to working-day jobs taking about 3.5% longer against plan for each standard deviation of rain days above normal, with no clear change in the chance of a late penalty.


Weather is measured at county level, so each job's weather carries error, which would bias these results toward zero. They are associations, not causal estimates. Hurricanes and other regional shocks can raise both rain and repair work.

Step 15 · TextText block 8: paste into the post body (Rich Text)145 words
Florida DOT ties weather relief to the work affected. Its statewide inspection guide for time extensions (2016-17) says extensions for weather, holidays and special events may be granted only when 50% or more of the predetermined controlling work items are affected, or when major repairs to weather damage affect that share. Rules differ by owner and by edition, so check the version your contract references.


Counting rules differ everywhere. Nguyen and colleagues list the definition of normal weather, weather thresholds, lingering days and the difference between work days and calendar days lost among the causes of discrepancy. The Society of Construction Law's Delay and Disruption Protocol, whose second edition appeared in 2017, is the common reference for determining extensions of time and compensation, and it is free to download. An owner reporting 49% delay and another reporting 13% may be describing the same performance.

How often are Texas DOT jobs late, and what do penalties cost?


One in six active jobs is past its allowed days


A snapshot of active jobs gives a different view from completed contracts. Texas DOT monthly progress reports list active state-let construction contracts with their allowed days and days charged. In January 2017, 17.1% of 1,519 active jobs had used more than all of their allowed days. In November 2024 the figure was 16.4% of 2,112 jobs. About one job in six was past its time on both dates, even though the live book of work grew from $14.5 billion to $40.7 billion in contract value, before any adjustment for inflation.

About a third of active jobs had used under 25% of their time on both dates (33.9% and 32.2%). About a quarter had used 75% to 100% (26.2% and 26.9%). The late share did not rise as the live book of work nearly tripled in value.


Late penalties are common but small


Texas DOT contracts set a daily penalty rate, and each penalty equals days late times that rate. Across 10,041 completed construction contracts, 22% were charged late penalties. The median penalty was 21 days and $18,000, which is 0.7% of contract value. All penalties together came to $109 million on $57 billion of contract awards.

What do public project risk registers cover?


A risk register is the working list of what could go wrong on a project and what the team will do about it. Mastt analysed the latest public version of 64 project risk registers, 53 Australian and 11 from the United States, published by councils, regulators, state parliaments and transport agencies or released under freedom of information.


88% list community or stakeholder risks, the most common theme. Approvals and permits appear in 83%, weather and flooding in 75% and heritage and culture in 72%. Cost escalation shows up in 55%, contamination in 52% and ground conditions in 50%. Utilities (39%), scope and design change (39%) and supply chain or labour (36%) appear far less often.

The gaps say as much as the common themes. Only 9 of 64 registers, 14%, list a contractor or subcontractor going insolvent. ASIC's Corporate Insolvency Update of October 2026 lists construction as the most common industry for appointments in 2025-26, at 24.5%, in a year when 14,153 companies entered external administration for the first time. Only 3 of the 18 Australian registers dated 2022 or later list the risk, and 7 of those 18 come from one network company. Where a fix is given, it is usually a financial check of the contractor at tender.


Climate change appears in 6 of the 64 registers but as a risk to the project in only 3, or 5%. The other three mention it in a definitions legend or as the project's own emissions. None of the 64 mention cyber risk, and 1 in 5 still mention COVID or pandemics.

What does an unchanged risk register look like?


One public hospital bond program in Washington State publishes its risk table inside each monthly status report. Mastt compared the table across the six 2024 reports it holds, for January, March, April, May, November and December. The risk text is identical in all six. The same 17 risks appear word for word, typos included. Two risk numbers are used twice, and no risk is marked closed at any point.

How long do approvals and delivery take?


NYC school change orders


NYC School Construction Authority records show how long a change takes to approve. Of 5,850 change issues in a November 2021 snapshot, 1,095 had reached an approved change order. The median time from a change being raised to its approval was 224 days. A quarter took longer than 516 days, and one in ten took close to 1,000 days. In Queens the median was 396 days. Late but under budget


Schedule and budget can move in opposite directions. Of 134 finished NYC school construction phases, 65% ended after the date originally planned. The median late finish was 100 days past plan, and 32 phases ran more than a year late. Yet the median final estimate came in 7.8% under the award budget, and 81% of these phases finished under budget. Design phases looked similar, with 49% late and a median 9.9% under budget.

New York State Thruway


On the Thruway, 304 completed contracts took a median 327 days from letting to completion. A quarter took under 212 days and a quarter took more than 486. Time rose with contract size, from a median of 264 days under $1 million to 673 days for the 19 contracts between $10 million and $100 million. That is a benchmark for a single state authority, and it measures from the letting date, not from the start of design.


Victorian state projects


Victorian state budget documents show how often finish dates move. Across 293 projects that appear in two or more budget years, 47% had their completion date pushed back. The typical slip among those was 4 quarters, and about a quarter slipped by more than 8 quarters. The Victorian Auditor-General's Office reviewed 110 major projects worth $149.33 billion in a report tabled in March 2026 and found that the state budget document for the capital program does not always include meaningful information about changes to cost, scope and completion dates. That gap limits how reliable any count of slipped projects can be, ours included.

FAQs


Short answers, each led by the figure.


How often does Texas DOT excuse rain days when gauges show little rain?


On 47% of the 2,170 weekdays Texas DOT credited for rain, county gauges showed under a tenth of an inch on the day or the day before, and 32% were fully dry. That is a signal rather than proof, because site conditions can lag rain and gauges can miss patchy storms.


Do wetter seasons cause more late penalties on Texas DOT jobs?


No. 12% of working-day contracts were charged late penalties in both the driest and the wettest fifth of seasons. The three middle fifths were higher, at 16% to 18%, so there is no straight-line pattern.


Why does Florida DOT show 13% delay on one measure and 49% on another?


The 431 contracts in Florida DOT's 2025-26 workbook used 48.7% more days than planned, but 12.5% more once 32,701 weather days and 19,228 holiday days were removed. The adjustment removes 74% of the extra days.


What share of active Texas DOT jobs are past their allowed days?


About one in six. 17.1% of active jobs in January 2017 and 16.4% in November 2024 had used more than all of their allowed days.


How big are Texas DOT late penalties?


The median penalty was $18,000, or 0.7% of contract value, and 22% of 10,041 completed construction contracts were charged one. Total penalties of $109 million compare with $57 billion of contract awards.


Which risks do project risk registers leave out?


Only 14% of 64 public registers list contractor insolvency, 5% treat climate change as a project risk and none mention cyber risk. By contrast 88% list community risks and 83% list approvals.


How long do NYC school change orders take to approve?


The median was 224 days among 1,095 approved change orders in a November 2021 snapshot, and a quarter took longer than 516 days. In Queens the median was 396 days and in Brooklyn 166.


Sources and methodology


Our own figures come from Mastt analysis of public records. These include more than 30,000 Texas DOT contract time and payment records, Texas DOT daily time charges matched to county rain gauge records, a state workbook of 431 Florida DOT contracts for 2025-26, Texas DOT monthly progress reports for two dates, 64 published project risk registers, New York City school construction records covering 5,850 change issues and more than 13,000 project phases, Thruway capital project records, and Victorian state budget documents for three budget years.


Texas figures describe Texas DOT work only. Completed contract figures use final estimates on construction contracts unless stated. Weather figures match each job to county rain gauge records over its work window and compare it with the normal for the same dates. A late penalty is a charge for days used beyond allowed days, in US dollars. Recent completed jobs skew toward shorter projects because long jobs are not yet final.


Risk theme counts come from keyword rules applied to the latest version of each register, with matches checked by hand where stated. The hospital program comparison is a line by line text comparison of six 2024 reports and a text recognition read of four 2025 reports. Victorian figures use three machine-readable budget years. Findings are associations and signals, not proof of cause.
